# On Slow Mornings and Good Broth

There is a particular kind of quiet that only exists before eight in the morning, when the kettle hasn't started singing yet and the light is still deciding what color it wants to be. I have come to believe that broth belongs to this hour. Not because it takes long to make, though it does, but because it asks for a patience that mornings are uniquely suited to give.

I start the broth before I have fully decided what I am cooking. Bones, water, a slow flame, and then I leave it. This is the part people rush past when they read recipes: the leaving. But the leaving is the whole point. It is an hour where nothing is required of you except to occasionally lift a lid and remember that something is happening, slowly, without your help.

I used to think productivity meant doing more in less time. Broth taught me otherwise. It taught me that some things cannot be sped up without losing what makes them worth having. The collagen needs its hours. The flavor needs to be coaxed, not forced. And somewhere in that stretch of doing very little, I usually find myself doing something else entirely: sitting by the window, writing a few lines, watching the neighborhood wake up in pieces.

I think we are taught to distrust stillness, as though it were laziness wearing a disguise. But a good broth cannot be made in a hurry, and neither, I am learning, can a good life. Both ask you to trust the process even when there is nothing visible to show for the first forty minutes. Both reward you only if you stay.

By the time the broth is ready, the morning has usually caught up to itself. The light has picked its color. The kitchen smells like something worth waking up for. And I ladle it into a bowl, not because I am especially hungry, but because I want to taste the proof that slowness, given enough time, becomes something nourishing.

Maybe that is the quiet lesson hiding in the pot: not everything needs to be quick to be worth doing. Some things are only good because you let them take their time.
