title: Things I Think About While Chopping Onions

Chopping onions gives you exactly enough to do with your hands to free the rest of your mind, and exactly enough discomfort to keep you present. I think this is why some of my clearest thoughts arrive over a cutting board, eyes stinging, knife finding its rhythm without needing supervision.

Today I thought about how strange it is that the same task can be soothing and irritating at once. The onion resists you a little, layer by layer, and yet there is something meditative in that resistance, in learning exactly how much pressure the knife needs, in the small satisfaction of a clean, even dice. Maybe most meaningful work feels like this: a little bit of sting, a little bit of rhythm, and a result you only fully appreciate later, once it's folded into something else.

I thought, too, about how much of cooking is actually about preparation rather than the final dramatic moment of plating. Nobody photographs the onions. Nobody writes essays about the garlic being minced. But the whole dish depends on this unglamorous stretch of labor, the part that happens quietly before anyone is watching. I find that oddly comforting. It reminds me that most good things are built the same way — through unremarkable, repeated effort that nobody applauds, long before the result becomes visible.

By the third onion, my mind had wandered somewhere else entirely, to an old conversation I hadn't resolved, to a plan I hadn't quite committed to. Chopping seems to invite this kind of drift. Maybe it's the repetition. Maybe it's the tears, which strip away a little of the performance we usually keep up even when alone.

Eventually the onions are done, and whatever I was thinking about gets set aside along with the knife. But I've noticed that the thoughts I have while chopping tend to be the honest ones, the ones I don't dress up for anyone. There is something to be said for a task humble enough to let your mind wander somewhere true.
