The hardest recipe I ever had to learn wasn't a dish. It was portion. Somewhere between cooking for a family that no longer needed feeding and cooking for just myself, I had to relearn what a meal was supposed to look like when there was only one plate at the table.

At first, cooking for one felt like a diminishment, as though the smaller quantities meant the effort mattered less. I would default to the same three meals, eaten standing at the counter, more refueling than eating. It took a while to notice what I was actually communicating to myself in that habit: that I wasn't worth cooking for properly.

Something shifted when I started setting the table anyway. Just one placemat, one glass, sometimes a candle if the evening called for it. It felt strange at first, almost performative, cooking a full meal for an audience of one. But the strangeness wore off, and what replaced it was something closer to self-respect. I was no longer treating solitude as a smaller version of company. I was treating it as its own occasion.

Cooking for one also means becoming honest about your own taste, without anyone else's preferences to negotiate around. I discovered I like my soups saltier than I ever let on when cooking for others. I discovered I have no real love for garlic bread, despite years of making it because someone else did. There is a specific clarity that comes from cooking only for yourself: the food finally tells the truth about what you actually enjoy.

I won't pretend the quiet doesn't sometimes feel too quiet, the chopping board too big for one person's dinner. But I have come to see this chapter of cooking for one as its own kind of practice, not a placeholder before company arrives, but a genuine, complete way of living. The meal is not smaller because it is solitary. It is simply its own size, made for its own purpose, worth the same care regardless of how many chairs are pulled out.
